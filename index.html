01

National Service Sentiment Analysis

Built during a 10-week Data Science (NLP) internship at MINDEF Singapore · May — Jul 2026

A nine-stage NLP pipeline and seven-page dashboard measuring how Singaporeans actually talk about National Service, plus a retrieval chatbot that answers questions over the whole corpus.

the problem
The department wanted to understand public sentiment on NS at a scale no one could read manually. Reddit had years of it — but 13.6M unstructured records written in Singlish and code-switched English, where off-the-shelf sentiment tools score badly and nobody could say how badly.
how i approached it
I started by distrusting the metric. Instead of training a classifier and reporting held-out accuracy, I hand-annotated a 217-row gold set first, then made four approaches prove themselves against it — VADER, a generic BERT, zero-shot GPT-4.1-mini, and a fine-tuned SingBERT that handles Singlish. Only the winner got used. The pipeline in front of it follows the same logic: semantic chunking before topic modelling, so UMAP and HDBSCAN cluster units of meaning rather than whole threads.
tools
PythonPyTorchHuggingFaceSingBERTspaCyUMAPHDBSCANFAISSStreamlitGroq
result
84% accuracy at κ = 0.71 and macro F1 0.78, against baselines of κ = 0.31 (VADER), 0.52 (generic BERT) and 0.68 (GPT-4.1-mini). 13.6M records reduced to 737K structured units and a 359-topic taxonomy. The chatbot routes quantitative questions to a pre-aggregated fact table and answers in under 100ms without calling a model at all.
what i learned
The expensive part of an ML project isn't training — it's building the thing you measure against. The gold set took days and was the only reason any accuracy figure meant anything. I also learned when not to call a model: routing stats questions to a lookup table made the chatbot faster and more honest at the same time.

02

Joga Bonito live

World Cup 2026 prediction engine · used by public visitors

A full 48-team bracket simulator with match-level predictions and squad exploration, served from a trained model rather than a rules table.

the problem
Most football models output a single win probability, which tells you nothing about how a match goes. They're also systematically biased toward home teams — a real problem at a World Cup where most fixtures are on neutral ground.
how i approached it
I modelled scoring rates instead of outcomes, so the classifier emits Poisson λ values you can simulate scorelines from. Two corrections came out of being honest about the data rather than clever with the model: squad quality is a strong signal but historical squad data doesn't reach far enough back to train on, so the 18-feature squad layer is applied at inference; and since home bias is measurable, every neutral-ground prediction is averaged with its own reversed fixture. Dixon-Coles calibration then fixes the low-scoring games Poisson reliably gets wrong.
tools
Pythonscikit-learnFastAPINext.js 14VercelRender
result
Trained on 49,214 international matches with four-year half-life decay. Two-stage K-means groups players into 20 archetypal roles. Brackets resolve across 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations. Live in production, with 2,256 matchups precomputed at startup to hide a free-tier cold start.
what i learned
Where you put a feature matters as much as whether you include it — moving squad quality out of training and into inference was the difference between a signal I couldn't use and one I could. And that engineering around a 30-second cold start was cheaper than paying to remove it.

03

Valedictorian Run live

Study platform with cited answers · live on Vercel

Upload a semester of course PDFs and get topic wikis, spaced-repetition cards, mock exams, teach-back grading and Q&A where every answer cites the page it came from.

the problem
Tools that run RAG over your own notes drift. The retriever returns the wrong chunk, you tune relevance thresholds indefinitely, and the answer cites nothing you can actually check against the source.
how i approached it
I asked whether the retrieval step needed to exist at all. One student's notes for one semester is a bounded corpus — small enough to sit in context. So instead of embedding and searching, the model reads each file once at ingest, extracts page-cited chunks and builds a topic index; Postgres full-text ranking is a fallback for when the window overflows, not the main path. Figures are extracted with mupdf and linked to topics, so the chat can read a diagram and still cite its page.
tools
Next.js 16TypeScriptSupabasePostgreSQLTailwindVercel AI SDKmupdf
result
Page-level citations on every answer, no vector database and no relevance tuning. Row-level security isolates each user's material. Cross-session search, image occlusion, SM-2 spaced repetition and Anki export. Runs at $0/month on free tiers.
what i learned
The default architecture isn't always the right one. RAG is the reflex answer to "chat with your documents", but for a bounded corpus it bought drift and tuning work in exchange for nothing. Deciding to remove a component is a design decision worth defending in the same way as adding one.

04

Context Aware Risk Engine prototype

Traffic accident detection under changing conditions · Feb — Mar 2026

An accident-detection system that quantifies how risky the current environment is, rather than trusting an object detector to decide on its own.

the problem
Detection-only systems alarm constantly in rain, fog and heavy traffic. Operators stop trusting them — alarm fatigue — and by the time a real incident happens, nobody's watching.
how i approached it
The core insight was that a fixed confidence threshold can't hold when conditions change, so the threshold has to move. 10,000 Monte Carlo runs establish a Value-at-Risk baseline for the current environment, Bayesian fusion combines that baseline with what the camera reports, and an alert only fires after consecutive breaches instead of a single frame. For perception I used zero-shot CLIP with engineered semantic anchors — "crumpled metal" against "normal traffic" — so it classifies what a scene means rather than counting objects inside it.
tools
PythonPyTorchCLIPOpenCVNumPyStreamlit
result
Three automated stress tests in a headless testbench: nominal traffic baseline, false-positive suppression under extreme weather, and crash-detection reliability. A Streamlit dashboard lets you push environmental parameters around and watch the threshold respond. Prototype — validated on the testbench, not deployed.
what i learned
The cheapest accuracy win is often temporal, not architectural: requiring two consecutive breaches killed more false positives than any model change I tried. I also learned that a demo you can't stress-test is only a demo, which is why the testbench got built before the interface did.

05

Bobina prototype

Conversational voice agent for sentiment interviews · built at MINDEF

A voice agent that conducts structured sentiment interviews over the phone or in a browser, transcribing, analysing and redacting as it goes.

the problem
Survey text boxes produce shallow answers. A human interviewer gets depth but doesn't scale, and the recordings then need transcribing and coding by hand. I finished my main internship project with two weeks left and wanted to see whether the depth could be automated without losing the conversation.
how i approached it
I built it as a streaming pipeline rather than a request-response loop, because turn latency is the difference between a voice agent that feels alive and one that feels broken — speech in, Groq for reasoning, Azure Neural streaming back out, orchestrated on Pipecat. The parts that took longest weren't the model: confidence-gated transcription so silence isn't scored as an answer, an idle watchdog that reminds and then hangs up, consent verification before anything is recorded, and NRIC, phone and email scrubbed from every transcript.
tools
PythonFastAPIPipecatWhisperGroq Llama 3.3Azure Neural TTSTwilioSQLite
result
0.2–0.9s from the end of a caller's sentence to the start of a reply, with ~200–300ms TTS streaming latency. Adaptive follow-ups fire only on strongly-held opinions. Graceful barge-in, automatic language switching, live SSE stream to an admin dashboard. Prototype on a free tier — around ten full interviews a day.
what i learned
Most of the engineering in a conversational system lives in the failure cases — silence, interruption, someone walking away mid-call. The model was the easy part. I also learned to build the privacy handling first instead of bolting it on, because redacting after the fact means the raw data already exists somewhere.

06

DSTA BrainHack TIL-AI finalist

Six AI challenge tracks, two people · Advanced Track Finalist, 2026

A national AI competition spanning gameplay agents, multilingual speech recognition, object detection, retrieval QA, adversarial attack and strategy — all under a hard compute budget.

the problem
Six unrelated problems, one teammate, a finals deadline, and constraints of one CPU and 1GiB RAM with under ten seconds per turn. Breadth was the actual challenge — any one track could have absorbed the whole timeline.
how i approached it
We split by track and iterated instead of designing up front. The gameplay agent went through sixteen numbered versions, each one a specific hypothesis about what was costing us reward — base rush, then phased, then offence-only, then the staged assault that finally worked. Keeping them as separate versions rather than edits to one file meant I could attribute every improvement to a cause. For the finals we put all five models behind a single routing endpoint on Docker Compose, so a submission was one HTTP call instead of five.
tools
PyTorchQwen3YOLOv8TensorRTONNXDocker ComposePettingZoo
result
Advanced Track Finalist. The gameplay agent reached 237.8 average reward, 7.4% above the v4 baseline. YOLOv8 fine-tuned across 19 military classes and exported to TensorRT at FP16. A checkerboard adversarial perturbation at ε=8 that stayed inside the SSIM constraint.
what i learned
Sixteen versions of one agent taught me more than any single clever idea would have. Disciplined versioning turns iteration into evidence — without it you improve a score and never know which change did it.

how i work

If there's one thing I'd want to be judged on, it's this. The projects change; the method doesn't.

  1. 01

    research — decide what would convince you, first

    Before I build anything I write down what I'd have to see to believe it worked, and what a fair baseline looks like. That ordering matters: choose the metric after the results and you'll pick the flattering one. On the NS project it meant hand-annotating 217 examples and benchmarking four approaches against them before committing to one. The gold set cost days and made every number afterwards defensible.

  2. 02

    build — thinnest end-to-end path, then attack the weakest stage

    I'd rather have an ugly pipeline where all nine stages run than three perfect ones and a gap. Once it runs end to end, the bottleneck tells you where to spend time instead of you guessing. Then I look for components that can be deleted rather than optimised — Valedictorian Run has no vector database and the NS chatbot's query router makes no model call, both because the version without them was simpler and measurably better.

  3. 03

    feedback — keep versions, not tweaks; find the failure before a user does

    Every significant change becomes a numbered version with a stated hypothesis, so an improvement can be traced to a cause. Sixteen versions of the BrainHack agent is what turned iteration into evidence. In parallel I build the thing that tries to break it early: CARE's stress testbench and Bobina's idle-then-hang-up handling both existed before either project had a usable interface.
